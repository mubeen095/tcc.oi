How to get an AI internship in 2026.

Most students apply to hundreds of internships and hear nothing. The ones who get offers usually did three unglamorous things first. Here is the sequence, in order.

The Coding Company 19 October 2026 8 min read

If you are a student reading this in 2026, you have already noticed the pattern. The internships that look most exciting are the ones with a thousand applicants, and the ones you can realistically win are the ones nobody told you about. Closing that gap is not about luck. It is a sequence, and almost everybody does it in the wrong order.

The mistake almost every student makes

The default student strategy is: apply first, build later. You write a resume listing courses, you send it to forty companies, and you wait. When nothing comes back, you assume you need more courses. So you take more courses, and the resume gets longer, and the results do not change.

The problem is sequencing. An internship application is a claim, and the reviewer's only job is to decide whether the claim is backed by evidence. Courses are not evidence. Deployed work is. Everything below is ordered so that the evidence exists before you start applying.

Step 1 — Pick one narrow target role

"AI internship" is not a role. Hiring managers do not have a budget line for it. They have a budget line for a data analyst intern, a marketing automation intern, a QA intern who can write test generation prompts, a junior developer who can work with an AI coding assistant.

Pick one of those. It should be a role that exists in companies you can actually name, and ideally one that connects to something you already study. If you are a commerce student, an AI-flavoured analyst role is a shorter bridge than an ML research internship. Use the industry hubs to see what roles exist in your field and what the AI version of each one looks like.

Narrow beats broad

A student who says "I want to be an AI-assisted financial analyst" gets taken seriously faster than one who says "I want to work in AI". Specificity is not a limitation — it is the thing that makes you referable.

Step 2 — Build three things, and deploy all three

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that decides everything. Three projects, each deployed to a live URL with a public repository. Not notebooks. Not certificates. Things a stranger can open in a browser and use.

  1. A tool that solves a real, boring problem in your target domain. If your target is financial analysis, build something that reads a company's filings and summarises the risk sections. Boring and useful beats clever and useless.
  2. Something that uses an AI model in a non-obvious way — retrieval over your own documents, an agent that calls two tools, a workflow that saves someone an hour a week. The reviewer is looking for judgement, not for a novel algorithm.
  3. Something that shows you can ship: a live URL, sensible README, a commit history that is not one giant dump on the last day. This is the project that gets you the interview even if the other two are imperfect.

Write each one up in 150 words: what problem, what you built, what you would do next. That write-up becomes the top of your resume and the script for your interview answers. If you want a longer runway before this step, a role-specific credential ladder such as the ones on TCC CERT+ is designed to end in exactly this kind of artifact rather than in a multiple-choice exam.

Step 3 — Make the application easy to say yes to

With the work in place, the application stops being a plea and becomes a short, factual email. Structure matters more than prose here.

  • One line on the role you are applying for, named exactly as the company named it.
  • One line on what you built that is relevant to that role, with the live link.
  • One line on why this company — something specific enough that it could not have been sent to anyone else.
  • One ask: a 15-minute conversation, or consideration for the internship. Not both, not five.

The whole thing should fit on a phone screen without scrolling. Most internship emails fail because they ask the reader to do work: to open an attachment, to infer relevance, to guess what you want.

Step 4 — Go warm, not wide

Cold applications have terrible odds for students, and it is not personal — internship pipelines are often filled before they are posted. Warm paths are what actually work, and they are more available than students think.

  • Alumni from your college who now work at companies you are targeting. One message, one specific question about their role.
  • People who wrote or built something you used in one of your three projects. Reference the thing, ask a real question about it.
  • Community events and webinars in your city. A five-minute conversation after a session routinely outperforms a hundred sent applications. TCC runs regular sessions — see the webinars page.

"Nobody hires an intern they have never heard of. Ten real conversations will outplace a hundred perfectly formatted applications."

What to do when you keep getting rejected

Rejection at the application stage usually means the evidence was not visible. Rejection at the interview stage usually means you could not explain your own project clearly. Those are different problems and they get different fixes.

If applications are not converting, the fix is upstream: deploy one more project, tighten the target role, or switch from cold to warm outreach. If interviews are not converting, the fix is rehearsal — you should be able to talk for five uninterrupted minutes about the design decisions in your own build, including the ones you got wrong. This is the same gap that placement programs exist to close.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get an AI internship with no experience?

Build three deployed projects in one narrow target role before you apply anywhere. Each should have a live URL, a public repository and a 150-word write-up. Then apply through warm paths — alumni, people whose work you used, and community events — rather than cold applications, which have very low odds for students with no experience.

Do I need a degree to get an AI internship?

Not always, but you do need visible evidence of ability. Deployed projects with public repositories demonstrate more to a hiring manager than a degree title alone. A role-based AI certification can help by naming the job title you are targeting, but the portfolio is what converts.

How many projects should I have before applying for AI internships?

Three is the practical threshold where applications start converting: one that solves a real problem in your target domain, one that uses AI in a non-obvious way, and one that proves you can ship — live URL, sensible README, credible commit history.

Why do cold applications for AI internships fail?

Internship pipelines are frequently filled through referrals and internal networks before they are publicly posted, and reviewers need to infer relevance from an attachment. Warm outreach — alumni, people whose work you used, and event conversations — consistently outperforms cold applications.

How long does it take to prepare for an AI internship?

Most students need a focused run of several weeks to build the three projects and write them up properly. That preparation is what makes the application work; repeating courses without deploying anything does not shorten the path to an offer.
